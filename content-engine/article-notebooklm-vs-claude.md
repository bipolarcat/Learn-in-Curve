# NotebookLM vs Claude Projects: which one to open when you've got 20 minutes

**By Sim · 13 August 2026 · ~7 min read**

All prices and limits checked 13 August 2026 against gemini.google/gb and claude.com/pricing.

---

I pay for both NotebookLM and Claude Projects. Not because I can't decide — because they do different jobs, and I only worked out where the line sits after building quiz questions for 24 learning objectives.

If you manage projects, you have the pile. Contracts, specifications, meeting minutes, lessons-learned logs nobody has opened since the project closed. Both of these tools promise the same fix: point AI at the documents, get answers back.

The useful question isn't which one is smarter. It's which one you open on a Tuesday afternoon with twenty minutes before a meeting.

## What you're actually buying

Start with the thing most comparisons skip: neither of these is really a product you buy on its own.

**Google AI Pro is £18.99 a month.** NotebookLM sits inside it — Google's UK page now lists it as "Gemini Notebook" — alongside Gemini 3.1 Pro, Google Flow with 1,000 credits, Nano Banana, entry-level access to Google Antigravity, Gemini inside Gmail and Docs, YouTube Premium Lite and 5TB of storage. There's also a cheaper Google AI Plus tier at £4.49 a month with lower limits, which is the sensible door in if you want to try the notebook properly before committing.

**Claude Pro is $17 a month billed annually, or $20 monthly.** That gets you Claude chat, Claude Code and Cowork.

Notice the currencies. Google gives you a UK price. Anthropic doesn't publish one at all, so you're billed in dollars and what actually leaves your account depends on the exchange rate that day plus whatever your card adds on top. It's a small thing. It compounds over a year, and nobody writing these comparisons from California is going to mention it.

## The actual difference

**NotebookLM answers questions *about* your sources, and won't wander outside them.** Fifty sources per notebook on the free tier, three hundred on Pro. Audio and video overviews are included, and you can interrupt the audio one to ask a question mid-flow — which sounds like a gimmick and isn't.

**Claude Projects works *with* your documents and hands something back** — a draft, a document, a decision. Two hundred thousand tokens of context, roughly five hundred pages, expanding about tenfold through retrieval when a project outgrows it. Thirty megabytes per file, unlimited files. The knowledge base needs a paid plan.

That distinction — answers about, versus works with — is the whole thing. Everything below follows from it.

## Where each one hurts

### Claude meters you

Roughly forty-five prompts per rolling five-hour window on Pro, and that pool is shared across chat, Claude Code and the desktop app. Anthropic doubled those limits on 6 May 2026, so it's better than it was, and it still bites when you're mid-flow on something large.

The problem isn't the money. It's that you start rationing your iterations — and iteration is the entire point of working this way. The moment you're mentally budgeting prompts, you stop exploring and start economising, and the output gets worse.

### NotebookLM charges you upfront, in effort

You have to gather the sources before you get anything back. For a genuine research job — a contract you need to interrogate, a syllabus you need to master — that's a fair trade and the payoff is real.

For "I just want to understand this thing," it isn't. I end up on YouTube, or just talking to Gemini, rather than assembling a source pack for a question I'll have answered in ten minutes.

That's not a missing feature. It's the setup cost being higher than the payoff for casual learning, and it's worth naming honestly because it's the reason most people who try NotebookLM don't stick with it.

## The worked example

Learn in Curve covers 24 learning objectives from the APM PMQ syllabus. I needed quiz questions for every one of them.

The obvious move is to put everything into one notebook and ask for questions.

That's the wrong move.

NotebookLM's defining strength is that it stays inside its sources. Lean into that rather than fighting it: I built **24 separate notebooks**, one per learning objective, each loaded only with that objective's material and given explicit instructions — *this is the source of truth, do not go off topic, produce quiz questions based only on what's in here.*

The questions came back on point every time. Cross-contamination between objectives never happened, because structurally it couldn't. There was no material from objective 14 sitting in the notebook for objective 3, so there was nothing to bleed.

However — I didn't ship them on trust. Every question went back through Claude to check it actually made sense as a question: unambiguous stem, one defensible answer, distractors that were wrong for a reason.

NotebookLM generated. Claude verified.

That's the relationship in one line, and it's more useful than any feature table.

## So which one

**If you have a deadline, go to Claude.** Two hundred pages of contract and twenty minutes: feed it in, state the time constraint explicitly in the prompt, give it your guidelines, and tell it to ask you questions rather than make assumptions. Those questions are what makes twenty minutes work. You spend the first three minutes answering them and the remaining seventeen getting something you can use.

**If you have time to learn, go to NotebookLM.** Gather the sources properly, constrain it hard, and it will not wander. The audio and video overviews are genuinely precise when you give clear guidelines — and if you need to retain the material rather than just skim it, the notebook format beats a chat window.

An honest caveat, because it changes how you should read all of the above: I reach for Claude because I know exactly how to drive it. Six to eight hours in NotebookLM against daily Claude use. Some of that preference is merit. Some of it is muscle memory. If you're starting fresh, that tilt doesn't apply to you, and you may well land somewhere different.

## The thing nobody says

You're not choosing between two tools. You're choosing between two bundles.

£18.99 buys a notebook, an image model, a video tool, an agentic development platform and five terabytes of storage. Roughly $20 buys depth on one workflow.

Neither is wrong. They're different bets — breadth against depth — and most comparisons never mention that the unit of comparison isn't the tool at all.

If you're a project manager deciding where twenty pounds a month goes, that's the framing that actually helps.
